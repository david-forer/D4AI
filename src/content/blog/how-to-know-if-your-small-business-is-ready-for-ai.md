A small business is ready for AI when it can name one repeated job, someone can describe the steps, the information lives somewhere a tool can reach and one person will own the result. Most businesses with 5 to 20 people are closer than they think, and the usual gap is a missing write-up.

This page gives you five signs that you are ready, four gaps that mean you should fix the work first and a 10-minute test you can run on a single job today.

## Five Signs Your Business Is Ready for AI

### You Can Name the Job

Readiness starts with a specific job. Picture a 9-person property management company where the office manager re-keys maintenance requests from email into a spreadsheet and then texts the right vendor. That is about 6 hours a week, it happens every week and everyone can point to it. "Triage maintenance emails" is a job you can hand to a tool.

### Someone Other Than You Can Describe the Steps

If the only person who can explain the job is the one who does it, the work lives in one head. AI cannot read a head. A sales coordinator at a 12-person staffing firm who follows up on every new candidate the same way, in the same order, can usually write that order down in 20 minutes. If she can't, that is the first thing to fix.

### The Inputs Sit Somewhere a Tool Can Reach

AI needs to see the material it works on. A job order in HubSpot, an invoice in QuickBooks or a request in a shared Google Sheet all work. A handwritten note on a job ticket does not, until someone types it up. Ask where the information is when the job starts and whether that tool can export it.

### You Can Say What a Good Result Looks Like

A good result for the maintenance example is a vendor texted within 15 minutes with the unit number and the problem in one line. That is a result you can check against a clock.

### One Person Will Own It Afterward

Systems nobody owns drift. Name the person who will notice when the output goes wrong and fix it. At a small firm that is often the office manager or the founder, and it should be settled before anything gets built.

## Four Gaps That Mean Fix the Work First

Each gap below maps to one of the signs above and tells you what to do next.

**The process lives in one person's head.** Write it down before you do anything else. This is the most common gap in founder-led firms, and a written process is useful even if you never touch AI.

**The information is on paper or in inboxes.** A bookkeeping firm whose clients email scanned PDFs of receipts has data, but not in a form a tool can read quickly. Fixing the intake step often comes before any automation.

**There is no rule on what customer data can go into AI tools.** Staff are probably pasting client details into free chatbots already. Write a one-page rule on which tools are approved and what data stays out. The [AI governance guide for small businesses](/blog/ai-governance-for-small-businesses) covers how.

**Nobody has the time.** If your team is at full capacity every week, a new system adds work for the first month before it removes any. Plan for that, or pick a smaller first job.

## A 10-Minute Test You Can Run Today

Take the job that cost your team the most hours last week. Then work through it in order.

1. Write the steps as a numbered list, from the trigger to the finished result. Count them.
2. Mark which steps follow fixed rules and which need judgment.
3. Next to each step, write where the information comes from.
4. Hand the list to someone who does not do the job and ask if they could run it from the page.

If the list runs past 15 steps or the other person can't follow it, the job needs mapping before AI will help. If it holds up and most steps follow fixed rules, you have a candidate. That is what the [AI Readiness Snapshot](/ai-readiness-snapshot) scores for the whole business in about 5 minutes if you want a second read.

## What You Can Skip Before You Start

You don't need clean data. The first pass finds out what state your data is in. You don't need a technical person on staff, because the first job is usually a connection between tools you already pay for. And you don't need a large budget. A single process audit costs $750.

## What to Do With Your Result

If you can name the job and the test holds up, start with that one job. [Where to start with AI in a small business](/blog/where-to-start-with-ai-small-business) lays out how to pick it and what to build first.

If you found gaps, close the smallest one this month. ## Questions Founders Ask About AI Readiness

### How Do I Know If My Small Business Is Ready for AI?

You are ready if you can name one repeated job, describe its steps on paper, point to where its information lives and name one person who will own it. Run the 10-minute test above on a single job to check.

### How Long Does It Take to Get Ready?

For most businesses with 5 to 20 people, 2 to 4 weeks of part-time effort covers the first job. Writing down the steps is usually the longest part.

### Do I Need an AI Strategy Before I Start?

No. Start with one job and measure it. A strategy comes from what the first few jobs teach you, and the [AI strategy guide for small businesses](/blog/ai-strategy-for-small-businesses) covers the sequencing once you have results.

### Is a Business With Under 10 People Too Small?

No. Smaller teams often move faster because fewer people have to agree on the process. The risk at that size is one person holding all the knowledge, which the 10-minute test exposes quickly.
